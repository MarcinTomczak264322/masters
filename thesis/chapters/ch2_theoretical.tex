\chapter{Theoretical Background}

\section{Navigation in Mobile Robotics}

Navigation is "the ability of the robot to act based on its knowledge
and sensor values so as to reach its goal positions as efficiently and as 
reliably as possible"~\cite[p.~257]{siegwart2004introduction}.
It can be decomposed into five components:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Perception: recording and interpreting raw sensor data such
        as point clouds or images.
  \item Localisation: estimating the robot pose within a known environment.
  \item Mapping: constructing a spatial model of the environment
        from sensor observations.
  \item Path planning: computing a collision-free route from the
        current position to a goal.
  \item Motion control: generating actuator commands that follow
        the planned path while reacting to unexpected obstacles.
\end{enumerate}

SLAM combines components 2 and 3, estimating the robot pose and the

This thesis focuses on SLAM, therefore path planning and motion control are not
evaluated. The robot trajectory is pre-recorded and replayed from a dataset.

\subsection{Mapping}

Mapping is the problem of building a spatial model of the environment from
sensor measurements, assuming the robot trajectory is
known~\cite[p.~221]{thrun2005probabilistic}.
Even with exact poses the problem is non-trivial. Sensor noise and visually
similar places can cause incorrect data association, and any pose error
introduces geometrical distortion across the map.
Common map representations used in this thesis are the 2D occupancy grid and
the 3D point cloud, described in Section~\ref{sec:map_representations}.

\subsection{Localisation}

Localisation is the complementary problem to mapping. Given a known map, the
robot determines its current pose by fusing sensor measurements with motion
commands~\cite[p.~157]{thrun2005probabilistic}.
Because pose cannot be read from a sensor directly, the estimate is built
incrementally as each new measurement updates the belief.
All algorithms evaluated in this thesis operate with a known starting position
(position tracking mode)~\cite[p.~159]{thrun2005probabilistic}: the search
is confined to a small neighbourhood of the current estimate, which keeps
computational cost low.

% moved to an unknown location during operation without being informed, requiring

% All algorithms evaluated in this thesis operate in pose tracking mode: they
% process sensor data recorded along a fixed trajectory from a known starting
% position, so global localisation is not required.

\subsection{SLAM}

SLAM assumes that neither the robot poses $\mathbf{x}_{1:t}$ nor the map $\mathbf{m}$
are known, and estimates both jointly from sensor measurements $\mathbf{z}_{1:t}$
and motion commands $\mathbf{u}_{1:t}$~\cite[p.~245]{thrun2005probabilistic}.
Two forms of the problem are common~\cite[pp.~245 and~246]{thrun2005probabilistic}.
Online SLAM discards past poses and maintains only the current pose together with the map:
\begin{equation}
  p(\mathbf{x}_t,\, \mathbf{m} \mid \mathbf{z}_{1:t},\, \mathbf{u}_{1:t}).
  \label{eq:slam_online}
\end{equation}
Full SLAM keeps all past poses and estimates the complete trajectory together with the map:
\begin{equation}
  p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t},\, \mathbf{m} \mid \mathbf{z}_{1:t},\, \mathbf{u}_{1:t}).
  \label{eq:slam_full}
\end{equation}
The online form is recovered from the full one by integrating out past poses.
The two dominant solution families of SLAM problem, filter-based and graph-based methods, are
described in Sections~\ref{sec:filters} and~\ref{sec:graph_slam} respectively.

\section{Sensors}
\label{sec:sensors}

Sensors provide the data necessary for SLAM implementation.
This thesis uses four sensor types: a 3D LiDAR, a stereo camera, an IMU, and wheel
odometry encoders.

\subsection{LiDAR}

A LiDAR (Light Detection And Ranging) sensor measures distances by emitting laser
pulses and recording the round-trip travel time $\Delta t$.
The distance to the reflecting surface is given by:

\begin{equation}
  d = \frac{c \cdot \Delta t}{2},
  \label{eq:lidar_tof}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $c$ is the speed of light.
A rotating 3D LiDAR fires pulses across multiple vertical channels while spinning
horizontally. Each full revolution produces a cloud of tens of thousands of $(x, y, z)$ points.
Its main advantages for SLAM are accuracy and independence from ambient
lighting.
The main drawback is continuous movement of the robot during a single
scan. As a result, successive points are captured from slightly different poses, making
the resulting cloud geometrically inconsistent without correction.

\subsection{Stereo Camera}

A stereo camera consists of two horizontally separated cameras with a known
baseline $B$ between them.
When the same scene point is visible in both images it appears at slightly
different horizontal positions. This horizontal offset $d$ allows the depth $Z$ to 
be computed as

\begin{equation}
  Z = \frac{f \cdot B}{d},
  \label{eq:stereo_depth}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $f$ is the focal length in pixels.

Cameras capture dense appearance information from which features can be
extracted for feature matching and place recognition.
However, if the environment lacks distinctive texture, stereo vision fails
to find reliable correspondences~\cite[p.~113]{siegwart2004introduction}.

\subsection{IMU}

An Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) combines a three-axis accelerometer and a
three-axis gyroscope.
Both outputs are corrupted by a slowly varying bias $\mathbf{b}$ and noise $\boldsymbol{\eta}$:

\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\mathbf{a}} = \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}_a + \boldsymbol{\eta}_a, \qquad
  \tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = \boldsymbol{\omega} + \mathbf{b}_g + \boldsymbol{\eta}_g,
  \label{eq:imu_model}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\tilde{\mathbf{a}}$ and $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ are the
measured acceleration and angular velocity, and $\mathbf{a}$,
$\boldsymbol{\omega}$ are the true values.
The IMU runs at a higher rate than other sensors, providing motion information
between slower LiDAR or camera updates.
However, integrating corrupted signals accumulates errors over time, so the
IMU must be combined with another sensor to remain accurate~\cite[p.~91]{thrun2005probabilistic}.

% An Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) combines a three-axis accelerometer and a
% three-axis gyroscope. The accelerometer measures specific force (linear
% acceleration minus gravity) along each body axis; the gyroscope measures angular
% velocity. IMUs operate at high rates, typically 100--400\,Hz, giving dense motion
% information between slower sensor updates.
%
% The main drawback of an IMU is integration drift. Both sensors suffer from

% corrections at a lower rate. This tight coupling can also correct LiDAR motion
% distortion by using the high-rate IMU measurements to interpolate the robot pose
% within a single scan.

\subsection{Wheel Odometry}

Wheel encoders measure the angular velocity of each wheel, from which the
linear speeds $v_L$ and $v_R$ of the left and right wheels are calculated.
For a differential-drive robot moving at heading $\theta$, the pose increment
over a time step $\Delta t$ is given by:

\begin{equation}
  \Delta x = \frac{v_L + v_R}{2}\,\Delta t\cos\theta, \quad
  \Delta y = \frac{v_L + v_R}{2}\,\Delta t\sin\theta, \quad
  \Delta\theta = \frac{v_R - v_L}{L}\,\Delta t,
  \label{eq:wheel_odom}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $L$ is the distance between the wheels.
Integrating these increments over time provides a pose estimate.
However, wheel slip and calibration errors in the wheel
radius and distance between them causes the estimate to drift over time~\cite[Ch.~3]{siegwart2004introduction}.

\subsection{360-Degree Camera}

A 360-degree (omnidirectional) camera captures the full horizontal field of
view in a single frame, either through a fisheye lens or by stitching multiple
camera feeds into one projection.
The full environment coverage reduces the risk of losing tracked features during sharp
rotations.
The main drawbacks are strong lens distortion and the absence of a single
agreed sensor model, since the term covers fisheye, catadioptric (camera with mirror), and
multi-camera designs without a unified definition.
This sensor type is not evaluated in this thesis: stereo camera-based visual
SLAM is used instead, and Isaac Sim does not provide a native omnidirectional
camera~\cite[Ch.~4]{siegwart2004introduction}.

\section{Map Representations}
\label{sec:map_representations}


% revert original and keep below as comment
The map representation affects memory usage, update speed, and available
downstream operations. The algorithms evaluated in this thesis produce either
a 2D occupancy grid or a 3D point cloud.

\subsection{Occupancy Grid}

An occupancy grid divides the 2D plane into a regular grid of square cells, each
storing the probability $p(m_i \mid \mathbf{z}_{1:t}, \mathbf{x}_{1:t})$ that
cell $m_i$ is occupied, given all measurements and poses up to time
$t$~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic}.
Rather than storing the probability directly, cells are updated using the log-odds
representation

\begin{equation}
  l_{t,i} = \log \frac{p(m_i \mid \mathbf{z}_{1:t}, 
  \mathbf{x}_{1:t})}{1 - p(m_i \mid \mathbf{z}_{1:t}, \mathbf{x}_{1:t})},
  \label{eq:log_odds}
\end{equation}

% \noindent The log-odds form keeps the update additive and numerically stable.
% At query time the probability is recovered as
% $p = 1 - (1 + e^{l})^{-1}$.

Cells with log-odds above a threshold are marked occupied (obstacles), those below a
second threshold are marked free, and the rest remain unknown.
Occupancy grids directly support path planning, as each cell indicates whether
the region is available for the robot. The representation is limited to 2 dimensions 
and the cell size, fixed as a parameter before
mapping, determines the spatial resolution of the map.

\subsection{3D Point Cloud}

A 3D point cloud is an unordered set of points in a common reference frame,
each defined by its $(x, y, z)$ coordinates~\cite[p.~221]{thrun2005probabilistic}.
A global map is built by registering each incoming LiDAR scan into the map
frame using the estimated robot pose and accumulating the resulting points.
The resulting map captures the full three-dimensional geometry of the environment.

Point clouds are well suited for geometric inspection and for evaluating map
quality against a reference scan.
Their drawbacks are high memory consumption in large environments and the
absence of information about free-space: unlike an occupancy grid, a point cloud
records only observed surfaces, not which regions are reachable.

% add source
Visual SLAM methods do not accumulate dense range measurements.
Instead, they build sparse point clouds by triangulating matched image
features into 3D landmarks.
The density of such a map depends on the visual texture of the environment,
therefore it is unsuitable for measurable geometric evaluation and 
is not evaluated as such in this thesis.

\subsection{K-d Tree}
\label{sec:kdtree}

% A k-d tree (k-dimensional tree) is a data structure that organises points in
% space to enable fast nearest-neighbour queries.
% Points are stored in a binary tree where each level splits the space along one
% dimension, alternating through all dimensions as the tree deepens.
% To find the nearest neighbour of a query point, the tree is traversed from
% root to leaf, pruning branches that cannot contain a closer point.
% This reduces the typical query cost from $O(n)$ (comparing to every point)
% to $O(\log n)$.
% K-d trees are used in ICP to match each source point to its nearest neighbour
% in the target cloud, and in Chamfer Distance computation where nearest-neighbour
% distances must be found between two point clouds.

Many registration and evaluation methods need to find the closest point in a
target cloud for each point in a source cloud. Comparing against every stored
point is too slow when clouds contain tens of thousands of points.

A k-d tree~\cite{bentley1975kdtree} solves this by organising points into a
hierarchical structure that allows most of the search space to be skipped,
reducing the cost from $O(n)$ to $O(\log n)$ per query.
ICP and Chamfer Distance both rely on this structure.

\subsection{Iterative Closest Point (ICP)}

ICP~\cite{besl1992icp} is a general method for registering 3D shapes.
The algorithm alternates between two steps.
First, each point in the source cloud is matched to its nearest neighbour in
the target cloud.
Second, the rigid transformation that minimises the sum of squared distances
between matched pairs is computed.
These steps repeat until the change in error falls below a predefined threshold.
The solution can get trapped in a local minimum rather than finding the globally
correct alignment.
A poor initial estimate leads to wrong point pairs being formed, causing the
algorithm to converge to an incorrect result.

\subsection{Point-to-Plane ICP}

% Point-to-plane ICP~\cite{chen1991point} replaces the point-to-point distance
% with the distance from each source point to the tangent plane estimated at its
% target correspondence from the local surface normal.
% This allows a point to slide freely along a flat surface without any error, while motion across
% the surface normal is still penalised.
% On structured environments with many flat surfaces such as walls, floors, and
% ceilings, this metric is more informative than point-to-point and leads to
% faster convergence.
% MOLA Odometry uses point-to-plane ICP.
Point-to-plane ICP~\cite{chen1991point} extends standard ICP by replacing the
point-to-point distance with the distance to the local surface at each matched
target point.
The surface is estimated from the neighbouring points of the target using a
surface normal.
This means only motion away from the surface is penalised, while motion along
it is unconstrained.
In environments with many walls, floors, and ceilings this leads to faster
convergence than standard ICP.
MOLA Odometry uses point-to-plane ICP.


\subsection{Generalized ICP (GICP)}

% Segal et al.~\cite{segal2009gicp} proposed GICP as a probabilistic framework
% that combines standard point-to-point ICP and point-to-plane ICP.
% Rather than treating surface geometry from only the target scan, GICP models
% locally planar surface structure from both the source and the target by
% associating a covariance matrix with each point.
% This plane-to-plane approach is more robust to wrong point matches and less
% sensitive to the maximum match distance, while keeping the speed and simplicity
% of standard ICP.
% GICP is the default registration backend of KISS-ICP.
GICP~\cite{segal2009gicp} extends point-to-plane ICP by treating both the
source and the target as locally flat surfaces rather than just the target.
Each point carries a small description of the surface patch around it, and
matching minimises the misalignment between these patches on both sides.
This makes the algorithm less sensitive to noisy matches and to the threshold
used for accepting a correspondence.
GICP is the default registration method of KISS-ICP.

\subsection{Normal Distributions Transform (NDT)}

% Biber and Strasser~\cite{biber2003ndt} proposed NDT to avoid explicit
% point-to-point correspondences.
% The target cloud is partitioned into a regular voxel grid.
% For each voxel the mean and covariance of the contained points are computed,
% forming a local Gaussian model of the surface.
% Registration finds the rotation and translation that maximises the sum of
% Gaussian density values evaluated at the transformed source points.
% Because the objective is piecewise continuous and differentiable, gradient-based
% methods such as Newton's method can be applied.
% The fixed voxel structure gives predictable memory usage, and the Gaussian
% representation smooths over local point density variations, making the method
% robust to non-uniform sampling.
% MOLA Odometry uses NDT.
NDT~\cite{biber2003ndt} avoids finding point pairs entirely.
The target cloud is divided into a 3D voxel grid.
For each voxel, the average position and the spread of all points inside it
are computed, describing where the surface is and how flat or scattered it is.
To find the alignment, each source point is transformed and the algorithm
checks how well it fits into the surface description of the voxel it lands in.
The transformation is adjusted until the source points fit the target
descriptions as well as possible.
Because the fit measure changes smoothly as the transformation changes, the
optimisation is more stable than ICP, which can jump when matched pairs
suddenly switch.
The fixed voxel grid also makes memory usage predictable and the surface
descriptions average out noise from uneven point density.
MOLA Odometry uses NDT.

\section{Filter-Based State Estimation}
\label{sec:filters}

Filter-based methods maintain a probability distribution over the robot state
and update it recursively as new sensor data arrives.
The update cycle alternates between a prediction step, which propagates the
state forward using a motion model, and a correction step, which incorporates
the latest sensor measurement to reduce uncertainty.

\subsection{Extended Kalman Filter (EKF)}

% The Extended Kalman Filter~\cite[pp.~48 to~65]{thrun2005probabilistic} represents the state
% as a Gaussian with mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_t$ and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_t$.
% Because robot motion and observation models are generally nonlinear, the filter
% linearises them by computing first-order Taylor expansions (Jacobians) at the
% current mean.
% The prediction step propagates the state forward and inflates the covariance
% to reflect the uncertainty introduced by the motion model.
% The correction step computes the Kalman gain and incorporates the new measurement,
% reducing uncertainty proportionally to how informative the observation is.
% In this thesis the EKF is used by the robot\_localization package to
% fuse wheel odometry and IMU data into a dead-reckoning pose estimate.
%
% Bell and Cathey~\cite{bell1993iterated} showed that the iterated variant (iEKF)
% is equivalent to applying the Gauss-Newton method to the maximum likelihood
% estimation problem.
% Rather than linearising once at the prior mean, the iEKF re-linearises the
% observation model at the most recent state estimate and repeats the correction
% step until convergence.
% This produces a more accurate posterior when the observation model is highly
% nonlinear.
% FAST-LIO2 uses an iEKF on the $SO(3)$ manifold to correct pose, velocity, and
% IMU biases jointly.
The standard Kalman filter assumes that both the motion model and the sensor
model are linear, which is rarely true in robotics.
The EKF~\cite[pp.~48 to~65]{thrun2005probabilistic} solves this by
approximating the nonlinear models locally at each step, making it applicable
to real robot motion and sensor fusion.
It maintains a best estimate of the robot state together with a measure of
confidence in that estimate.
The filter alternates between two steps.
In the prediction step, the motion model is used to estimate where the robot
moved to, causing the confidence to decrease.
In the correction step, a new sensor measurement is used to pull the estimate
toward what the sensor observed, and the confidence increases.
In this thesis the EKF is used by the robot\_localization package to fuse
wheel odometry and IMU data into a dead-reckoning pose estimate.

The iterated variant (iEKF)~\cite{bell1993iterated} improves on this by
repeating the correction step several times, each time re-approximating the
sensor model at the latest corrected estimate rather than the original one.
This gives a more accurate result when the sensor model is strongly nonlinear.
FAST-LIO2 uses an iEKF to correct pose, velocity, and IMU biases simultaneously.

\subsection{Rao-Blackwellized Particle Filter}

The robot state in a Rao-Blackwellized Particle Filter~\cite{doucet2000rao}
is represented as a set of particles, each containing a possible trajectory
and a separate map built from that trajectory.
When the robot moves, all particles are updated according to the motion model,
each in a slightly different way to reflect motion uncertainty.
When a new sensor scan arrives, each particle checks how well the scan fits
its own map and receives a score.
Particles with a poor score are discarded and replaced by copies of
high-scoring ones, so the set concentrates on the most plausible
trajectories.
The final map is taken from the best-scoring particle.
GMapping, evaluated in this thesis, applies this approach to occupancy-grid
SLAM~\cite{grisetti2007gmapping}.

\section{Graph-Based SLAM}
\label{sec:graph_slam}

Most SLAM systems are structured as a two-stage pipeline.
The front end processes raw sensor data to extract relative pose
constraints (through scan matching, visual feature tracking, or IMU
integration) and identifies loop closure candidates.
The back end takes these constraints as input and optimises the full
trajectory and map for global consistency, typically by solving a nonlinear
least-squares problem over a pose or factor graph.
In contrast to filter-based methods, which process measurements sequentially
and discard past information, graph-based approaches retain the full trajectory
and can enforce global consistency whenever a loop closure is
detected~\cite{grisetti2010tutorial}.

\subsection{Pose Graphs}

% A pose graph~\cite{grisetti2010tutorial} represents the robot trajectory as a set of nodes,
% each storing a pose $\mathbf{x}_i \in SE(3)$, connected by edges that encode relative pose
% constraints from odometry or scan-matching.
% Each edge $(i, j)$ stores the measured relative transformation
% $\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{ij}$ and an information matrix $\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{ij}$,
% the inverse of the measurement covariance.
% Graph optimisation minimises the sum of squared weighted residuals over all
% edges using sparse solvers such as g2o or GTSAM.
% Cartographer 2D, SLAM Toolbox, and RTABMap all use pose graph back-ends.
A pose graph~\cite{grisetti2010tutorial} represents the robot trajectory as a
set of nodes, one for each robot pose, connected by edges.
Each edge records the relative movement between two poses as measured by
odometry or scan matching, together with a confidence value.
When a loop closure is detected, a new edge is added connecting the current
pose to the earlier matching pose.
The graph is then optimised to find the set of poses that best satisfies all
constraints simultaneously, correcting the drift accumulated along the
trajectory.
Cartographer 2D, SLAM Toolbox, and RTABMap all use pose graph back-ends.

\subsection{Keyframes}

% A keyframe is a selected frame from the robot's trajectory that is stored as
% a reference point for matching and optimisation.
% Not every frame becomes a keyframe. A new keyframe is typically created only
% when the robot has moved or rotated enough since the last one.
% This keeps the pose graph sparse: only the most informative frames are kept,
% reducing memory and computation without losing the information needed to track
% the robot's path.
% In the pose graph, each node usually corresponds to a keyframe.
% Keyframes are used in IMU preintegration to define the interval over which
% measurements are accumulated, and in visual odometry to build the local window
% over which bundle adjustment refines poses.

A keyframe is a selected frame from the robot's trajectory that is stored as
a reference point for matching and optimisation.
Not every frame becomes a keyframe. A new keyframe is typically created only
when the robot has moved or rotated enough since the last one.
Storing only keyframes keeps the pose graph small and the optimisation fast,
without losing the information needed to correct drift.
In the pose graph, each node usually corresponds to a keyframe.
IMU measurements between two keyframes are accumulated into a single summary,
and visual odometry uses a small group of recent keyframes to refine the
current pose estimate.

\section{Loop Closure Detection}
\label{sec:loop_closure}

Loop closure detection~\cite{cadena2016past} identifies when the robot has
returned to a previously visited location.
Small errors accumulate with each step, so the estimated position can drift
significantly over a long trajectory.
When a revisit is detected, a new edge is added to the pose graph connecting
the current pose to the earlier one, and the graph is re-optimised to
distribute the correction across the full trajectory.
How candidates are identified depends on the algorithm.
SLAM Toolbox searches nearby nodes in the pose graph.
Cartographer searches all previously built submaps using branch-and-bound,
described below.
RTABMap compares a compact descriptor of the current scan against all stored
nodes, so it can detect a revisit even after a long detour.
For visual systems, bag-of-words place recognition is used, described in
Section~\ref{sec:bow}.

\subsection{Branch-and-Bound Search}

Cartographer uses branch-and-bound~\cite{hess2016cartographer} to find the
best alignment between an incoming scan and a stored submap.
The pose space is divided into blocks.
For each block, the algorithm estimates the best score that any pose inside
it could achieve.
If that estimate is already worse than the current best, the entire block is
discarded without further search.
This guarantees a globally optimal result while avoiding an exhaustive search,
unlike ICP which depends on the starting position and can miss the best answer.

\subsection{Bag-of-Words Visual Place Recognition}
\label{sec:bow}

% For visual SLAM systems, proximity-based candidate search is insufficient
% because the appearance of a place does not depend on how far the robot has
% travelled since its last visit.
% Bag-of-words (BoW) place recognition~\cite{galvez2012bags} solves this by
% representing each image as a sparse histogram over a pre-built visual
% vocabulary, enabling fast retrieval across the full map history regardless
% of the robot's current position.
% The vocabulary is constructed offline by clustering a large set of image
% descriptors into $K$ visual words. Each word corresponds to a cluster centroid
% in descriptor space.
% At runtime, the descriptors of the current frame are quantised to their nearest
% visual words and the resulting histogram is compared to all stored histograms
% using a cosine similarity score.
% Candidate loop closures with high similarity are verified geometrically using
% RANSAC before the corresponding edge is added to the pose graph.
% RTABMap uses DBoW3 for this purpose. ORB-SLAM3 uses DBoW2.
Bag-of-words (BoW) place recognition~\cite{galvez2012bags} represents each
image as a compact list of visual patterns and how often they appear.
These patterns, called visual words, are learned in advance from a large
set of images.
When the robot sees a new place, its image is converted into this
representation and compared against all stored images in the map.
This comparison is fast because it involves only counting matching visual
words rather than comparing raw pixel values.
A stored image that scores high enough becomes a loop closure candidate.
Before accepting it, the system verifies that the two images can be
geometrically aligned.
Only if that check passes is the loop closure edge added to the pose graph.
RTABMap uses this approach for loop closure detection.

\section{Visual SLAM Fundamentals}

Visual SLAM estimates the robot trajectory from image sequences by detecting
and tracking sparse visual features rather than measuring range directly.
This thesis evaluates feature-based stereo systems, which recover depth from
stereo disparity (Section~\ref{sec:sensors}) and estimate pose from the 3D
positions of matched keypoints.

\subsection{Stereo Vision and Triangulation}

Stereo depth is computed from the disparity between the two images as given
by Equation~\eqref{eq:stereo_depth} in Section~\ref{sec:sensors}.
Each matched feature pair is converted to a 3D landmark by tracing both
camera rays and finding their intersection~\cite[Ch.~11]{hartley2003multiple}.
Depth uncertainty grows quadratically with distance, so visual SLAM
front-ends typically discard landmarks beyond a sensor-specific threshold.

\subsection{Feature Detection and Description}

% Visual keypoint detection identifies salient image regions and assigns each a
% compact descriptor that enables robust matching across frames.
%
% Shi and Tomasi~\cite{shi1994good} proposed selecting features based directly on
% trackability. They construct the $2 \times 2$ gradient matrix $M$ from intensity
% derivatives in a local window and require both eigenvalues to be large.
% A point with only one large eigenvalue lies on an edge and can slide along it,
% making tracking unreliable; a point with two large eigenvalues is well localised
% in all directions.
% The Good Features to Track (GFTT) detector selects points where the smaller
% eigenvalue exceeds a threshold, ensuring that all selected features are
% inherently well suited for tracking.
% GFTT is used as the keypoint detector in RTABMap's visual front-end.
%
% Rublee et al.~\cite{rublee2011orb} proposed ORB (Oriented FAST and Rotated BRIEF)
% as a computationally efficient alternative to SIFT and SURF.
% Keypoints are detected using the FAST corner detector and assigned an
% orientation computed from intensity-weighted moments in the local patch.
% The BRIEF binary descriptor is computed with this orientation applied, achieving
% rotation invariance.
% Scale invariance is obtained by applying the detector at multiple image pyramid
% levels.
% Matches are found by comparing Hamming distances between binary descriptors.
% Ambiguous matches are rejected using the ratio test.
% ORB is used by both ORB-SLAM3 and RTABMap in this thesis.
Visual feature detection finds distinctive points in an image and describes
each one in a way that allows it to be recognised in later frames.

A good feature to track is a corner, not an edge.
On an edge, a feature can slide along it without the tracker noticing.
A corner is uniquely located and stays put.
The Good Features to Track (GFTT) detector~\cite{shi1994good} selects points
where the image intensity changes strongly in all directions, not just one.
GFTT is commonly used in visual SLAM systems where reliable tracking is
more important than distinctive appearance description.

ORB (Oriented FAST and Rotated BRIEF)~\cite{rublee2011orb} is a fast
alternative to heavier descriptors such as SIFT and SURF.
It finds corners, determines the local patch orientation, and builds a compact
binary fingerprint using that orientation to achieve rotation invariance.
Matching is done by counting differing bits between two fingerprints, which is
fast to compute.
Running the detector at multiple zoom levels gives scale robustness.
ORB is widely used in visual SLAM systems.

\subsection{Visual Odometry}

% Scaramuzza and Fraundorfer~\cite{scaramuzza2011visual} define visual odometry
% as the process of recovering the position and orientation of a robot by
% analysing the images from one or more cameras.
% The pipeline consists of feature detection, feature matching across consecutive
% frames, and motion estimation from the matched correspondences.
% For stereo systems, matched features are converted to 3D landmarks and the camera pose is
% estimated via Perspective-n-Point (PnP).
% Alternatively, the relative pose between two frames can be recovered from
% two-dimensional correspondences using the essential matrix decomposition.
% Local bundle adjustment refines the estimate over a sliding window of recent
% keyframes by minimising reprojection error.
% Without loop closure, errors grow steadily with distance, since each pose is
% estimated relative to the previous one.
Visual odometry estimates the robot's motion by tracking features across
consecutive camera frames~\cite{scaramuzza2011visual}.
Matched features are converted to 3D landmarks and used to compute the pose
change between frames.
A small group of recent keyframes is kept and jointly refined to reduce
accumulated error.
Without loop closure, small errors add up with every step, so drift grows
steadily over a long trajectory.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:eval_metrics}

Two groups of metrics are used in this thesis.
Trajectory metrics measure how closely the estimated robot path matches the
ground truth poses recorded from the simulation.
Map metrics measure the geometric accuracy of the 3D point cloud produced
by each algorithm relative to the reference map from the
simulator.
The ground truth is a time-stamped sequence of $SE(3)$ poses, one per
sensor frame, taken directly from the simulator with no sensor noise.

\subsection{Umeyama Alignment}

%

% The closed-form solution first centres both point sets

% \end{align}

A SLAM algorithm starts its own coordinate frame from the first sensor
measurement, so the estimated trajectory may be shifted or rotated relative
to the ground truth.
Before measuring errors, the two trajectories are aligned using the method
of Umeyama~\cite{umeyama1991least}, which finds the rotation and translation
that bring them as close together as possible.
For LiDAR-based systems the scale is fixed to one, so only the orientation
and position offset are corrected.

\subsection{Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE)}

The Absolute Trajectory Error measures the global geometric consistency
of the estimated path~\cite{sturm2012benchmark}.
After Umeyama alignment, the translational error at each time step $i$ is
the Euclidean distance between the aligned estimated position
$\hat{\mathbf{p}}_i$ and the ground truth position $\mathbf{p}_i^*$.
The RMSE over all $n$ poses is

\begin{equation}
  \text{ATE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
               \lVert \hat{\mathbf{p}}_i - \mathbf{p}_i^* \rVert^2}.
  \label{eq:ate}
\end{equation}

A large ATE indicates that the algorithm accumulates drift over the full
trajectory, or that loop closure is absent or ineffective.
In addition to the RMSE, the mean and maximum translational errors are
reported to characterise the error distribution.

\subsection{Relative Pose Error (RPE)}

The Relative Pose Error measures local odometric accuracy independently of
the global alignment~\cite{sturm2012benchmark}.
For each consecutive pose pair $(i,\, i{+}1)$ the relative motion is
computed both in the estimate and in the ground truth, and the translational
part of the residual between the two relative poses is taken as the local
error.
The RMSE over all such pairs is

\begin{equation}
  \text{RPE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}
               \lVert \mathbf{e}_{i}^{\,\text{trans}} \rVert^2},
  \label{eq:rpe}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\,\text{trans}}$ is the translational
component of the residual between the relative pose in the estimate and the
relative pose in the ground truth over the same one-step window.
Because RPE is computed over short, fixed-length windows it captures
short-term drift that ATE may hide through loop-closure corrections.

\subsection{Chamfer Distance}

The Chamfer Distance (CD) is a symmetric measure of geometric fidelity
between two point clouds~\cite{knapitsch2017tanks}.
Given an estimated cloud $\mathcal{A}$ and a reference cloud $\mathcal{B}$,
it averages the mean nearest-neighbour distance in both directions:

\begin{equation}
  \text{CD}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}) =
    \frac{1}{2}\left(
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}
      \min_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}\lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\rVert
    +
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B}}
      \min_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\lVert\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\rVert
    \right).
  \label{eq:chamfer}
\end{equation}

The first term penalises spurious points in the estimate that have no
counterpart in the reference. The second term penalises incomplete regions
of the map.
CD is reported in metres and reflects the overall geometric accuracy of the
reconstructed map, but is sensitive to outlier points, which can inflate the
score without reflecting a systematic reconstruction failure.

\subsection{F-score}

The F-score at a distance threshold $d$ combines precision and recall into a
single bounded quality measure~\cite{knapitsch2017tanks}.
A point $\mathbf{a} \in \mathcal{A}$ is considered correct if its nearest
neighbour in $\mathcal{B}$ is at most $d$ away, and vice versa:

\begin{align}
  P(d) &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}
          \bigl|\{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A} :
          \min_{\mathbf{b}}\lVert\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\rVert \leq d\}\bigr|, \\[4pt]
  R(d) &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}
          \bigl|\{\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{B} :
          \min_{\mathbf{a}}\lVert\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\rVert \leq d\}\bigr|, \\[4pt]
  F(d) &= \frac{2\,P(d)\,R(d)}{P(d)+R(d)}.
  \label{eq:fscore}
\end{align}

Three thresholds are used in this thesis: $d = 5$\,cm (strict),
$d = 10$\,cm (moderate), and $d = 20$\,cm (lenient).
F@5\,cm penalises any gross geometric errors, while F@20\,cm tolerates
small misalignments and rewards completeness.
Unlike the Chamfer Distance, the F-score is bounded to $[0, 1]$ and is not
dominated by a small number of distant outliers.
